Science · grade 6

The water cycle

Follow one drop from the sea to the sky and back.

Ms. AlvarezLesson 4

Good morning. Today we follow a single drop of water on a long journey. By the end of the lesson you will be able to name the four stages of the water cycle, draw them in order, and explain why the same water has been circulating for millions of years. Please keep your notebooks open for the questions.

Start here

Where does the rain come from?

Give students one minute to talk in pairs. Collect three answers on the board without correcting them yet. Common answers are clouds, the sky and the sea. All of them contain a piece of the truth. Tell the class that by the end of the lesson they will be able to connect those pieces into a single story.

The cycle

Four stages, no beginning

Reveal the stages one at a time and ask students to predict the next one before you press. The sea warms and its water rises as vapour. High up it cools and gathers into clouds. When the drops get heavy they fall as rain, and the water runs into rivers that return to the sea. Then the cycle starts again.

Key terms

Three words to remember

Evaporation

Liquid becomes vapour.

Condensation

Vapour becomes drops.

Precipitation

Water falls to the ground.

These three words appear on the quiz, so read them aloud together. Evaporation is liquid water turning into vapour. Condensation is vapour cooling into tiny drops, which is why a cold glass gets wet on a warm day. Precipitation means any water that falls: rain, snow, sleet or hail. Ask for one everyday example of each.

Experiment

A cloud in a jar

  1. Pour hot water into a jar
  2. Cover it with a plate of ice
  3. Watch drops form underneath

Adults handle the hot water.

This experiment makes condensation visible in about two minutes. Hot water gives off vapour. The plate of ice cools the air at the top of the jar, and the vapour turns into drops that gather on the glass and run down, like rain. Students should predict what they will see before they try it, then compare.

Earth's water

Almost all of it is in the sea

97%salt water
2%ice and glaciers
1%fresh liquid water

Source: USGS Water Science School, rounded to whole numbers.

Rounded, about ninety-seven percent of the water on Earth is salty, around two percent is frozen, and only about one percent is fresh liquid water that people can drink. That is why the cycle matters: it moves a little salt water through the air and turns it into the fresh water that fills rivers and lakes.

The timeline is an illustration, not a measurement: real journeys vary from hours to thousands of years, depending on where the water goes. Ask students where the drop might be slower, for example frozen in a glacier, and where it might be faster. Use this to prepare the check on the next slide.

Check

Check your understanding

  1. Name the four stages in order
  2. Which word means vapour turning into drops?
  3. Why is fresh water so precious?
Give students three minutes to answer in their notebooks, then reveal one question at a time. Walk around and read answers. Praise the use of the key words, not only the right answer. Take the third question as a discussion: it connects the science to everyday life and leads naturally into the homework.

Homework

Draw the cycle for a place you know and label the four stages.

For homework, students draw the water cycle for a real place: their town, a holiday spot or a farm. They label the four stages and add one thing from the place, such as a river or a roof. Bring the drawings next lesson. Thank the class, and remind them to try the cloud in a jar with an adult.
