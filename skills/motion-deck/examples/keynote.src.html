The quiet web

Software that respects attention.

Lena ParkProduct design

Good evening. I want to talk about a quality that is hard to put on a roadmap: quiet. Not minimal, not empty. Quiet as in a product that lets you finish what you came to do. I will make an argument, show one number, share a short story, and end with something you can do on Monday.

Today

Every app wants to be the loudest in your pocket.

Start slowly. Think about the last time your phone buzzed during a meal, and what it was about. Almost always it was a product asking to be remembered, not a person asking for help. Each team that added a notification had a reasonable goal. Together they made a very loud place.

Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity.

Simone Weil
Simone Weil wrote this in a letter in 1942. It reminds us that attention is not a resource to harvest, but something people give. When a product respects it, the person feels trusted. When it does not, they feel handled. Let the sentence sit for a moment before moving on; do not explain it.

Part 1

What quiet looks like

Three habits of calm products.

Now the constructive part. I have looked at products that people describe as calm, and found three habits they share. None of them is about removing features. All of them are about deciding, on purpose, who is in control of the next moment: the product or the person.

Habits

Three habits of calm products

They wait

No alert without a reason.

They finish

Tasks end with a clear done.

They explain

Settings say what they cost.

Calm products wait: they only interrupt when something needs you now. They finish: a task has an ending, a clear done state, rather than an infinite feed. And they explain: every setting says what it will cost you in attention, so the choice is informed. These are design decisions, and they are cheap to make early.

One number

What a quiet default saved

62%fewer notifications sent
11%more weekly active users

Source: product analytics, a six-month test on one team (example figures; replace with yours).

On one product we changed the default from every notification to only the ones a person asked for. We sent sixty-two percent fewer messages, and weekly active use went up eleven percent. People came back because they wanted to, not because they were pulled. This is one test on one team, so treat it as a signal, not a law.

Part 2

Start on Monday

Small changes you control today.

You do not need a strategy change to start. Everything on the next slide is something one designer, one engineer or one product manager can do without asking for permission. I will keep it concrete, because the best intentions about attention tend to dissolve in the first sprint planning meeting.

This week

Four days, four small acts

  1. MonList every notification you send
  2. TueCut the ones nobody asked for
  3. WedAdd a clear done state
  4. ThuWrite what each setting costs
Monday: list every notification your product sends, in one table. Tuesday: remove the ones nobody asked for, and defend that decision with the table. Wednesday: pick one flow and give it a clear ending. Thursday: rewrite the settings text so it says what each option costs. By Friday you will have shipped a quieter product.

The ask

Build the product you would still like at midnight.

Here is the test I use. Would you still like this product if it contacted you at midnight, on a day you were tired? If the honest answer is no, something in it is asking for more than it gives. That is a design problem you can solve, and solving it is worth more than any growth hack.
